Getting started

  1. Open the editor. There is nothing to install and nothing to sign up for.
  2. Choose your level at the top. That sets the length you are aiming for, and the big timer turns green once you are inside it.
  3. Add files — or drag your music anywhere onto the page. Dropping songs onto the row of blocks adds them to the program at the same time.
  4. Pick a song on the left and press Add to program.
  5. Trim it, order it, blend the joins, watch the clock, and press Make music file when it sounds right.

Your music never leaves your computer. The files are read straight from disk by the browser, then decoded, mixed and encoded inside the tab and written back to your downloads. There is no server and no upload step.

The screen

Music, on the left, is everything you have opened. It is a shelf rather than the program — a song sitting here is not in your program until you add it.

Program, the row of blocks, is the running order. Click a block to work on that song; drag it left or right to move it.

The bar underneath is the real timing, overlaps included. Where two blocks overlap, that is a blend. The shaded stripe is the length you are allowed. Click anywhere on the bar to move the marker, then press Space to listen from there.

The clip editor appears below once a song is selected. Trimming, fades and blending all happen there, and they apply to that one song.

Trimming a song

Drag either end of the waveform. Or put the marker where you want the cut and press I to start there and O to end there — usually more accurate, because you can listen to the exact spot first. The arrow keys nudge the end point, and Shift moves it further.

Trimming never touches your source files. Nothing the editor does modifies the music on your disk. Trims are only numbers, so you can widen a cut back out at any time.

Play this song auditions the selected clip on its own, exactly as it is set — trimmed, at its own volume, with its fades. It is the quickest way to check a cut without playing the whole program up to it.

Ordering and blending

Drag blocks to reorder, or use Move earlier and Move later underneath — which is how to do it on a touchscreen, where dragging doesn’t work.

Blend into previous overlaps this song with the one before it, so one eases into the next. One or two seconds is usually enough. Leave it at 0 for a sudden, deliberate change.

Blending makes the program shorter, because the two songs overlap rather than follow each other. Watch the timer as you move the slider.

Fade in and Fade out are for the very start and the very end. About half a second in stops the music opening with a pop; two or three seconds out lets the ending finish rather than stopping dead.

Hear this join plays the few seconds either side of a cut, so you can judge it without hunting for the spot.

Where to cut

Cut where a line of the music finishes, not in the middle of one. If you can hear the join, it is usually in the wrong place rather than badly blended.

Lining up a join

Line up this join nudges a cut by up to a couple of seconds to somewhere the music can actually take it, then tells you what that did to the running total.

  • When both songs have a steady beat, it puts them both on it. Two songs whose beats disagree is what makes a blend sound like a stumble, and this is what fixes it.
  • Piano and much orchestral music have no steady beat to find. There it looks for the end of a phrase instead — the breath between one musical line and the next — and tells you that is what it did. Cutting there is what makes a join sound intended rather than accidental.
  • If it can find neither, it says so and changes nothing, and you place the cut by ear with I and O.

Ctrl+Z undoes it either way, so it costs nothing to try.

Volume

Even out the volume sets every song so they all sound about equally loud, without letting any of them distort.

This matters more than it sounds like it should. Records are mastered at wildly different levels, and the rink’s sound desk is set once and left alone — so a quiet cut in the middle of your program simply disappears.

Use it once your cuts are roughly right, and again if you re-trim, because it measures the part you kept rather than the whole song.

The per-song Volume slider adjusts it by hand afterwards. Evening out is a starting point, not the last word: a gentle opening is often meant to be softer than the finish.

The clock, and program lengths

The big timer shows the real length of the program, overlaps included. It turns green inside the allowed window for your event, and tells you exactly how far off you are when you are not.

Aim for the middle of the green range rather than the very edge, so a few seconds either way will not matter. Timing runs from the first sound to the last, so quiet passages still count.

Check the times yourself

The built-in times are a convenience, not an authority. Required lengths are set by the governing bodies and do change between seasons. Check your level against the current rulebook, or with your coach, before an entry deadline.

The list covers ISU singles, pairs and ice dance, and U.S. Figure Skating free skate and short program levels. Every option shows its time next to the level name, so a wrong number is visible rather than hidden, and there is a Custom length option for anything not listed.

This project is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or connected to the ISU or U.S. Figure Skating.

Sound quality labels

Each song gets a plain verdict rather than a bitrate you have to interpret.

Label What it means
Good This song will sound fine at the competition.
Fair It will do, but it won’t sound crisp on big speakers.
Low This may sound rough at the rink. Try to find a better copy.
Unknown We couldn’t check this one. It’s probably fine.

The numbers are easy to misread — a small bitrate for one kind of file can sound better than a big one for another, and a high-bitrate rip of a poor source is still a poor source. Hover a label in the app to see the detail behind the verdict.

Exporting at a higher quality cannot rescue a song that was already poor. You need a better copy of the song itself. The editor says so before you export rather than after — and once you have that better copy, you do not have to start the program again. See Swapping in a better copy.

Swapping in a better copy

You cut a program around a rough download, and then you buy the licensed version. Press Replace beside the song and choose the new file — or just drag it onto that song in the list. Every trim, blend, fade and volume you set stays exactly as it was. Only the file underneath changes.

Replace only appears on songs your program actually plays. A song nothing uses can simply be removed and a better one added, which is the same thing with fewer questions.

What it checks before it does it

This is the one thing the editor can do that goes wrong without looking wrong. Hand it the wrong song and every cut still applies, the clock still reads correctly, the strip still draws — and the program is now a careful edit of the wrong music. So it checks, and shows you every check:

  • Is it the same recording? Not the same file name and not the same tags, either of which can say anything. The editor compares how the sound rises and falls through the whole song, which is what a recording keeps when it is encoded again and what two different songs do not share.
  • Is it actually better? Compared the way the quality labels are, so a swap to a more efficient kind of file is not reported as a downgrade.
  • Does the music start in the same place? A fresh download often begins a fraction of a second later or earlier than the copy it replaces. The editor finds by how much and moves every trim to match, so your cuts stay on the same moments in the music.
  • Does everything still fit? If the new file is shorter, it says which clips will have to be shortened before it shortens them.

Nothing here stops you. If the checks are unhappy the button reads Replace anyway, because there are good reasons to swap in a copy that is no better — a licensed one, for instance — and only you know whether this is one of them. Nothing changes until you press it, and Cancel leaves everything exactly as it was.

The song you replaced stays in the list. It is not part of your program any more, so Remove is available on it once you are sure — and until then Ctrl+Z puts everything back the way it was, music included.

How much memory the music uses

The editor keeps every song in the list unpacked and ready, so you can move a trim and hear the result straight away. That costs about 85 MB for every four minutes of a song, and it costs the same whether your program uses ten seconds of it or all of it.

On a normal computer this never matters. On a small laptop or a Chromebook, a long list of long songs can use enough that the browser closes the tab — browsers do this without asking, and there is no warning first.

So the editor keeps count, and says something when the list is getting heavy. It will offer to give back any song that is not in your program, which is memory you are spending on nothing. Your program is saved as you work either way, so a closed tab never costs you your editing — but you would have to open all the music again, which is a bad ten minutes on a competition morning.

You can do the same thing yourself at any time: Remove beside a song takes it out of the list and gives its memory back. It is only available on songs your program does not use, and it never deletes anything from your computer.

What a file says about itself

Music files usually carry the title, the artist and often the composer, written in by whoever made the file. Press Info beside a song in the list to see what yours holds.

Where there is a title, it becomes the name of the song in the list and of any clip you cut from it — better than track03.mp3. The file name stays under it, because that is what identifies the file and what the editor has to find again later.

Two of the fields are worth knowing about if you need permission to use a piece of music. ISRC is a code that identifies one particular recording rather than the song, which is what a licensing service will ask for. Copyright usually names whoever owns it.

The panel has a link that looks the song up on ClicknClear, the licensing service the ISU points skaters to for clearing music rights. It searches for the title the file gives, so you are not retyping it. Whether you need to clear anything, and with whom, depends on your event and where you skate — ask your club or your federation.

Nothing is sent anywhere to read these. The tags are already inside the file, and the editor reads them on your computer along with the rest of it.

Saving and loading

The browser remembers your work between visits on its own, so you do not need to save after every change. Save project is for keeping a copy, moving to another computer, or being able to go back to an older version later.

A project file records your editing, not the music. It is a few kilobytes precisely because the songs are not inside it.

That has three consequences worth knowing before a competition:

  • It cannot be played on its own, and it cannot be handed in. Use Make music file for that.
  • When you load a project, you add the music files again. Use Add files, or drag them onto the page — anywhere will do.
  • Until the music is added, those songs show in red and the program will neither play nor export.

Songs reconnect by file name. As long as the names have not changed, every cut, fade and blend comes back exactly. Rename a music file and the link breaks — rename it back, or remove the song and add it again. Keeping the project file in the same folder as the music is the easy way to keep the two together.

Opening the music again in one click

On Chrome and Edge the editor can usually do this for you. Where the browser supports it, it remembers the songs you opened and offers Open the music again — one click for the whole program, instead of finding each song by hand.

The audio still never leaves your computer. The browser is only handing back a file you already chose, and it asks your permission before it does.

Firefox and Safari have no such facility, so there it works exactly as it always did: Add files, and the cuts come back. If a remembered song has been moved, renamed or deleted, the editor says which and points you at Add files rather than failing quietly.

The wrong song with the right name

Because songs are matched by name, the editor also checks that the song it has been handed is the same one — not a re-download at another quality, and not track01.mp3 from a different album.

Without that check the trims would still apply and the timer would still read correctly, and the whole program would quietly be built around the wrong music. If it spots a mismatch it tells you instead.

New

New starts a fresh program and asks for a name and an event again. Your loaded songs stay in the list, and nothing is ever deleted from your computer — but the current program is replaced, so save it first if you want it back.

The project file

Save project writes a small, readable JSON file:

{
  "$schema": "https://ankurs47.github.io/skate-program-editor/docs/program.skate.schema.json",
  "format": "skate-program",
  "version": 1,
  "name": "my 2026 junior long program",
  "event": {
    "level": "usfs-jr",
    "label": "Junior — 3:30 ±10s",
    "targetSeconds": 210,
    "toleranceSeconds": 10
  },
  "songs": [
    { "name": "chosen song.mp3", "bytes": 5216340, "seconds": 212.5,
      "fingerprint": "1k4v9p" }
  ],
  "clips": [
    { "id": "opening", "song": "chosen song.mp3", "title": "chosen song",
      "start": 4.76, "end": 77.08,
      "fadeIn": 1.5, "fadeOut": 0, "blend": 0, "gainDb": 0 }
  ],
  "notes": "coach wants the slow part a little longer"
}

The words are the ones the editor uses on screen. start and end are seconds into the song, blend is how long this clip overlaps the one before it, and gainDb is the level in decibels — the same number the volume slider shows, where 0 means the song as recorded.

songs records what each song was — not where it was. A browser will not tell a page where a file lives, so a project cannot save a location. What it saves instead is enough to recognize a different song wearing the same name, which is the check described above.

A file written by hand needs only clips: with no songs section, nothing is claimed about the music. Anything missing falls back to something sensible rather than refusing to open, so it is safe to edit.

version is the one thing that is not guessed at. A project saved by a newer editor than the one you are using will say so and decline to open, rather than showing you a program that looks right and is not.

notes is yours. Open Notes under the Program heading and write whatever is worth remembering — what your coach asked for, what to try next. It is saved with the project and shown again when you open it, and nothing else reads it. If you keep projects in git, as the read-me suggests, remember that whatever you write there is published along with them.

The $schema line points a text editor at a description of this format, so if you edit a project by hand you get the field names completed and mistakes underlined. It is only a convenience; deleting it changes nothing.

Making the music file

Make music file renders the whole program — trims, fades, blends and volumes — and saves it as MP3 or WAV.

If the program would come out too loud to store cleanly, it warns you before encoding rather than after. Turn the Volume down on the loudest songs, or use Even out the volume, which keeps everything below the limit.

Then listen on proper speakers before the competition. Laptop speakers hide problems the rink’s system will not.

Getting the music off YouTube

The editor works on files that are already on your computer, and often the song you want is one you have only ever heard on YouTube. The repository’s tools/ folder has a small helper for that — one script for Mac and Linux, one for Windows, doing the same thing: 

./tools/music-get.sh "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=..."     # Mac, Linux
tools\music-get.cmd "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=..."      # Windows
  -o DIR      where to put the files (default: here)
  --playlist  the link is part of a playlist and you want all of it,
              not just the one song

It saves the sound exactly as YouTube already stores it — usually a .webm file, which Chrome and Firefox open without complaint. Nothing is converted, so no quality is lost before you start cutting and there is no ffmpeg to install. Give it several links at once and it works through them in turn.

The one thing to install is yt-dlp, which does the actual work: 

brew install yt-dlp             # Mac
sudo apt install yt-dlp         # Debian, Ubuntu
winget install yt-dlp.yt-dlp    # Windows

The script says so, with the command for your system, if it is not there. YouTube changes often enough that a download failing usually means yt-dlp is due an update — yt-dlp -U.

Competition music is normally covered by the license your rink or federation holds, but that is between you and them; this tool only moves audio onto your own disk.

Browsers and devices

Any current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari on a computer. The page tells you if something it needs is missing, rather than failing quietly.

It works on a tablet but warns you first. Trimming by touch is fiddly, and decoded audio is uncompressed — roughly 90 MB of memory per four minutes of stereo — so a few long songs can exhaust what a mobile browser allows.

Running it yourself

Clone the repository and open index.html. That is the whole build process: no build step, and no runtime dependencies.

To host it, copy index.html and the src/ folder anywhere that serves static files. Because users’ music never reaches the host, there is no storage, no per-user cost, and no third-party audio on your infrastructure.

The page makes no outbound requests at all. Browsers can play MP3 but none of them can create one, so the editor carries its own encoder — mediabunny’s MP3 encoder, a WebAssembly build of LAME, sitting in src/vendor/ with the rest of the files. Once the page has loaded, everything it does works with the network switched off.

What it deliberately doesn’t do

  • Songs play in sequence. No layering, no second track, no EQ. A program is a sequence of cuts, and the tool is shaped around that.
  • It never modifies your source files.
  • It cannot improve a poor recording. Exporting at a higher quality will not rescue a song that was already bad.

When something goes wrong

What you see What to do
Songs in red, and the program won’t play That project’s music has not been added yet. Use Add files, or Open the music again if the button is there.
No Open the music again button Either the browser cannot do it (Firefox, Safari) or nothing was remembered for this project. Add files always works.
A song has moved, been renamed or been deleted The browser can no longer reach the file it remembered. Find it again with Add files.
This is a different song with the same name You have been handed another copy — a different album, or a re-download at another quality. Use the original file, or re-trim against the new one.
Line up this join changed nothing The music has neither a steady beat nor a phrase ending near that cut. Place it by ear with I and O.
A YouTube download fails yt-dlp is usually due an update — yt-dlp -U.
Export only offers WAV The MP3 encoder did not load. WAV is losslessly fine to hand in; it is just a much bigger file.
